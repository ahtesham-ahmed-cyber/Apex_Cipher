Service — Strategy

Decide where AI is actually useful

Most AI conversations begin with a tool and work backwards. We begin with the problem: what decision or task is currently slow, unreliable, or expensive, and whether the data and process around it can support an intelligent system at all.

The problem

AI is easy to demo and hard to depend on

A prototype can be built in a week. A system that a regulated team can rely on for critical work is a different problem entirely. Between those two points sit the questions we spend most of an assessment on: what counts as good output, what happens when the model is wrong, who reviews the result, and how the system is improved once it is running.

Our consulting engagements exist to answer those questions before significant engineering investment is committed, using your data, your processes, and the regulatory environment you actually operate in.

Useful? Task clarity, success criteria
Feasible? Data, integration, latency
Safe? Failure modes, oversight
Sustainable? Cost, operations, improvement

The four questions we work through before recommending any AI delivery work.

What we do

Four areas of AI consulting

Each of these can be delivered as a short standalone engagement. Most clients begin with an opportunity assessment and continue into planning or delivery work once the direction is clear.

01 — Assessment

Opportunity assessment

A structured review of where AI could plausibly change an outcome, ranked by feasibility and value.

Task discovery

Interviews and observation with the people doing the work today, not only the people sponsoring it.

Candidate long list

Every plausible AI use case we can identify, with rough effort and value estimates.

Feasibility screening

Data availability, quality, latency, and integration reality applied to each candidate.

Short list and rationale

A small number of well-argued candidates, with the reasons the others were set aside.

02 — Readiness

Readiness review

An honest look at whether your organization is set up to build, run, and improve an AI system today.

Data foundations

Where the necessary data lives, how trustworthy it is, and what access looks like in practice.

Integration surface

How a new system would connect to the tools your team already uses, including the awkward ones.

Governance and policy

Existing rules, review processes, and risk frameworks the new system must work within.

Team capability

What your engineers, analysts, and operators will need to run and evolve the system over time.

03 — Planning

Implementation planning

A concrete sequence of work with dependencies, decision points, and measurable checkpoints.

Architecture direction

The shape of the system, described at a level your engineers can act on.

Phased plan

Increments that each deliver working software and can be evaluated independently.

Evaluation strategy

What "good" looks like, how it will be measured, and who is responsible for the measurement.

Risk register

The known risks, their mitigations, and the signals that would tell us to change course.

04 — Responsibility

Responsible AI practices

The practices we build into every subsequent engagement, tailored to the consequences of the system being wrong.

Failure-mode analysis

What happens when the model is wrong, and how quickly that becomes visible.

Human oversight points

Where a person reviews, approves, or can intervene, and how those decisions are recorded.

Traceability

The ability to reconstruct how a specific output was produced, including the sources it drew on.

Ongoing evaluation

Task-specific test sets that catch quality changes over time, not only at launch.

How it runs

A short, focused engagement

A typical assessment runs for a few weeks and ends with a written recommendation. It does not commit you to any delivery work with us.

  1. 01

    Scoping call

    A working session with the people sponsoring the work to agree on the questions the assessment should answer.

  2. 02

    Discovery

    Interviews, data review, and system inspection with the teams who would live with the resulting system.

  3. 03

    Analysis

    Candidates are screened against feasibility, and a short list is developed with rationale.

  4. 04

    Recommendation

    A written report and a working session to discuss the findings, including the options we are not recommending.

Boundaries

What we will not do

A good assessment includes a clear view of what is not worth pursuing. These are the cases where we will tell you so directly.

Where we push back

We would rather deliver an honest recommendation than a deliverable that flatters the original proposal.

We will say no to

  • Recommending AI for a task where deterministic software is clearly better.
  • Proposing an agent that has unrestricted access to production data.
  • Committing to accuracy targets that cannot be measured.
  • Treating model choice as the first question rather than the last.
  • Handing over a system without a plan for who operates it.

We will invest the extra time in

  • Talking to the people who do the work today.
  • Building an evaluation set before choosing a model.
  • Documenting the options we did not choose and why.
  • Writing recommendations the engineering team can act on.
  • Being clear about the parts of the plan we are least certain of.

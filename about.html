Our position

A small team that stays on the build

We are deliberately structured so the people who scope the work are the same people who write and review it. There is no translation layer between the conversation with your team and the code that reaches production.

That constraint shapes everything else about how we work: the size of engagements we accept, the technology choices we recommend, and the level of documentation we leave behind. It also means we are honest about what we are not the right fit for.

Mission

Make technology legible

The systems that carry important work should be understandable to the people responsible for them — not only to the engineers who originally built them.

Why legibility matters

When a system is legible, teams can reason about it under pressure. They can diagnose a failure at three in the morning, estimate the impact of a change, and decide whether to invest in extension or replacement. Legibility is the difference between a platform that grows with the organization and one that becomes an obligation.

Understandable Changeable

Named owners

Every system we help build has an identified owner on your side before we hand it over. Ownership is a role, not a slide.

Written contracts

Interfaces, service boundaries, and data models are documented in the repository, not in a private document.

Reversibility

Decisions we cannot easily reverse get the most scrutiny. Where a decision is reversible, we choose the option that keeps momentum and revisit it in the open.

A quiet relationship with novelty

We are enthusiastic about new tools and genuinely cautious about new dependencies. The two positions are not in conflict. The question we return to is not “is this interesting?” but “does this make the system easier to operate in two years?”

Working philosophy

Five commitments that shape every engagement

These are not aspirations. They are constraints we hold ourselves to, including when they are inconvenient for the schedule.

Clarity over cleverness

We prefer the readable solution. A slightly less elegant implementation that a future engineer can reason about is worth more than a clever one that requires tribal knowledge.

Evidence over opinion

Performance budgets, test coverage, evaluation datasets, and observability metrics turn debates about quality into measurements the whole team can inspect.

Transferred, not retained

Success means the platform runs without us. We pair deliberately, review in the open, and treat the handover as part of the deliverable rather than a formality.

Security as a design input

Threat modelling, least-privilege access, and dependency hygiene belong at architecture time. Retrofitting them later is expensive and usually incomplete.

Honest about limits

If a problem is better solved by a different team, a different tool, or not at all, we say so. We would rather decline work than deliver something we do not believe in.

How we work

A delivery rhythm built on clarity and evidence

The sequence below applies to every engagement. The depth of each phase changes with the scope of the work; the order does not.

  1. 01

    Discover

    We map the current system, interview the people who run it, and agree on the outcomes that will define success.

  2. 02

    Architect

    We compare realistic options, document the trade-offs, and commit to an architecture the whole team understands.

  3. 03

    Build

    Short, reviewable increments with automated tests, continuous integration, and a working demonstration at the end of every cycle.

  4. 04

    Evolve

    We hand over with documentation, runbooks, and observability so your team can operate, extend, and improve the system independently.

Fit

Where we do our best work

We would rather be useful than broadly applicable. These are the situations where we consistently produce the strongest outcomes.

Good fit

Engagements where a small, senior team can work in close contact with the people who own the problem.

We tend to do well when

  • The problem is well-defined enough to scope but not yet solved.
  • There is at least one senior engineer on the client side to work alongside.
  • The organization is prepared to operate the system once it exists.
  • Requirements are expected to evolve and architecture must accommodate that.
  • Regulatory or security constraints are known and can be discussed openly.

We are likely not the right fit when

  • The primary need is staff augmentation for a long, unspecified period.
  • A vendor selection has already been made and no reconsideration is possible.
  • The work depends on outcomes no one on the team can influence.
  • There is no realistic path to internal ownership of the system afterwards.
  • The scope is expected to be delivered faster than it can be properly tested.
